Battle of the Stars

For school, projects, and research assignments

Stars are enormous physics laboratories in which gravity, energy production, and flows of matter play a central role. In Sterrenstrijd, you can explore these processes yourself with an interactive simulator of single and binary stars. By changing properties such as mass, distance, and evolutionary stage, you can investigate how stars develop and how they influence each other when they form a binary system. For example, you can see how gravity determines orbits, how matter can flow from one star to another, and how changes in mass affect a star’s further evolution.

The simulator connects to topics from the typical high school physics curriculum, such as gravity, energy, radiation, and the use of models. You can use it to deepen concepts from class, to formulate your own research questions, or as a tool for a project or final research assignment. Sterrenstrijd invites you to think like a physicist: formulate a hypothesis, adjust the parameters in the model, and see what happens. In this way, you discover how the laws of physics determine the behaviour of stars in the universe.

In short: this site is your science arena. You're not just reading about the stars - you're running experiments on them.

Project ideas

You can use the Sterrenstrijd simulators to carry out your own research on the behaviour and evolution of stars and binary stars. Below you will find example research questions that are well suited for a final school project or research project. In this case, you use the simulator as a model to predict and analyse the possible evolution of a system. These questions can serve as a starting point for your own research: by formulating hypotheses, systematically varying parameters, and studying the outcomes, you can discover how physical laws determine the behaviour of stars.

For each mini‑project, you can formulate a clear hypothesis, adjust the relevant parameters, compare the results, and draw conclusions. In doing so, you follow the same steps that real physicists use when they try to understand the life cycle of stars.

About stars

  • Which star evolves the fastest?
    Research question: How does the mass of a star influence the speed of its evolution?
    Method: Create stars with different initial masses and let them evolve. Note how quickly they pass through the evolutionary stages. Which star changes the fastest?
    Extension: Explore the theory behind stellar lifetimes and luminosities. Investigate how mass affects the speed of evolution and whether this matches your simulation.
  • Giants and dwarf stars
    Research question: In which phase does a star reach its largest radius, and how does this depend on its mass?
    Method: Create stars with different masses. Follow the radius during the evolution and record the maximum size and in which phase this occurs.
    Extension: Study the role of energy transport and pressure inside stars. Investigate why some stars become larger than others.
  • Which stars lose the most matter through stellar winds?
    Research question: How does the final mass of a star depend on its initial mass and mass loss via stellar winds?
    Method: Create stars with different initial masses and let them evolve. Note the difference between initial and final mass.
    Extension: Investigate how mass and evolutionary phase determine mass loss.
    Extra extension: Consider how metallicity influences this, and what happens to the expelled gas. Part of this material will later become part of gas clouds from which new stars form. In this way, mass loss through stellar winds is part of the “life cycle” of matter in the universe.
  • Stars in the early universe
    Research question: In the early universe there were hardly any metals and stars had low metallicities (Z ~ 0.0001 instead of Z = 0.02 in our current Milky Way). How does low metallicity affect the radius, luminosity, core mass, and final mass of a star?
    Method: Create stars with the same mass but different metallicities. Compare their evolution and final masses.
    Extension: Investigate how metals affect opacity and energy transport, and what consequences this has for the structure and evolution of stars.
  • Stars in the Milky Way, Large Magellanic Cloud, and Small Magellanic Cloud
    Research question: How does the difference in metallicity between the Milky Way (Z = 0.02), the Large Magellanic Cloud (Z = 0.01), and the Small Magellanic Cloud (Z = 0.005) affect the evolution and mass loss of stars?
    Method: Create stars with the same initial mass but with metallicities corresponding to the Milky Way, the Large Magellanic Cloud, and the Small Magellanic Cloud. Compare their evolution, radius, luminosity, mass loss through stellar winds, and final mass. Hint: Pay particular attention to massive stars, for which stellar winds strongly depend on metallicity.
    Extension: Explore why stellar winds are stronger at high metallicity (the role of metals in opacity and line-driven winds). Investigate the consequences for the final products (white dwarfs, neutron stars, black holes) in different galaxies, and connect your findings to observations of stars in the Magellanic Clouds.

About binary stars

  • How does evolution change in a binary system?
    Research question: How does mass transfer affect the evolution of a star in a binary system?
    Method: Compare a star in isolation with the same star in a binary system. Note differences in evolutionary timescale and final mass.
    Extension: Investigate how filling the Roche lobe and mass transfer affect the lifetime and luminosity of the star.
  • How does separation affect a binary system?
    Research question: What happens to the orbits and interactions of two stars when they are closer together?
    Method: Create binary systems with the same stellar masses but different separations. Observe how the orbits and interactions change. When does mass transfer occur?
    Extension: Investigate the role of interactions and the Roche lobe, and how this determines when mass loss begins.
  • Which binary stars merge?
    Research question: In the universe, mergers lead to bright outbursts that we observe in large numbers. Which binary systems are susceptible to merging?
    Method: Create binary systems with different masses and separations. Observe which systems merge and in which phase of their evolution this happens.
    Extension: Investigate the role of mass transfer, Roche lobe, and interactions in mergers.
  • Build your heaviest black hole
    Research question: How are a star’s mass and its metallicity related to the mass of the black hole that ultimately remains? Tip: focus especially on the effect of metallicity on mass loss through stellar winds.
    Method: Create massive stars with different initial masses and different metallicities (for example those of the Milky Way, Large Magellanic Cloud, and Small Magellanic Cloud). Let them evolve, record the final mass, and see which stars end as black holes and how massive they are.
    Extension: Investigate why stars with low metallicity lose less mass through stellar winds and can therefore form more massive black holes. Connect this to observations of heavy black holes in other galaxies and to gravitational waves.
    Extra extension: Extend your research to binary stars. How does the final mass of black holes change when stars are part of a binary system? Investigate in which cases interactions (such as mass transfer or common-envelope phases) lead to more massive black holes, and when they instead produce lighter black holes.
  • Build your lightest white dwarf
    Research question: What is the lowest mass of a white dwarf you can make from a single star, and can you create even lighter white dwarfs in binary systems by tuning the orbit?
    Method: First create single stars with different initial masses and let them evolve into white dwarfs. Note the lowest white-dwarf mass you can reach in this way. Then investigate binary stars: vary the orbital properties (such as the orbital separation) and see whether you can make stars lose their outer layers earlier so that lighter white dwarfs form.
    Extension: Look at the chemical composition of the white dwarfs you create (for example helium, carbon–oxygen, or oxygen–neon–magnesium white dwarfs). Investigate for which initial masses, metallicities, and in which (binary) situations these different types form, and explain why the composition is linked to the star’s evolutionary history.
  • Can you make a Type Ia supernova?
    Research question: Type Ia supernovae are extremely bright explosions that we use to measure distances and the expansion of the universe. How can a white dwarf become so massive that it exceeds its maximum mass (1.44 solar masses) and explodes?
    Method: Create a binary with a white dwarf and investigate two scenarios: mass transfer from a companion star, or a merger with another white dwarf. Note for which combinations the white dwarf reaches its critical mass.
    Extension: Study the Chandrasekhar mass and why this limit determines when a white dwarf explodes as a Type Ia supernova.
  • Can you create a source of gravitational waves?
    Research question: Since 2015, we have been able to detect gravitational waves. Which binary systems of compact objects can produce such signals?
    Method: Create binary systems of white dwarfs, neutron stars, or black holes and let them evolve. Note which systems become close enough and have short enough orbital periods to emit gravitational waves.
    Hint: For the LVK detector, orbital periods are often hours to minutes; for the LISA detector, days to months.
    Extension: Investigate how mass, separation, and orbital size affect the strength of the signal.
  • How old is a star cluster?
    Research question: A star cluster is a group of stars that formed together and have roughly the same age. How can you estimate the age of such a cluster with a simulation?
    Method: Create multiple stars with different masses that together form a cluster. Let them evolve and note in which evolutionary phase the most massive stars are. Use this to estimate the age of the cluster.
    Extension: Explore the role of the turn-off point in the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram and how it is used to determine the ages of real star clusters.

About exoplanets

  • What happens to Earth when the Sun evolves?
    Research question: How do the orbits of Earth and the other planets change as the Sun grows older and becomes a red giant?
    Method: Simulate the Sun with the planets of the Solar System one by one. Let the Sun evolve and observe how the planetary orbits change. Note which planets move closer, move farther away, or are at risk of being engulfed.
    Extension: Explore the red-giant phase and how mass loss from the Sun affects planetary orbits. Which physical processes determine the fate of the planets?
  • Can you “eat” a planet?
    Research question: Under what conditions can a star engulf a nearby planet?
    Method: Create a system with a star and a close-in planet. Vary the distance and mass of the planet. Observe how the orbit changes and when the planet comes close enough to be swallowed by the star.
    Extension: Investigate the role of tidal forces, the Roche lobe, and radiation pressure. Why is it harder for small planets to survive close to a star than for larger planets?
  • How do planets affect the evolution of a star?
    Research question: Can massive planets or gas giants influence their host star?
    Method: Add a massive planet or multiple planets around a star in the simulation. Observe how the planetary orbits change and whether this has any effect on the outer layers of the star.
    Extension: Explore how the mass and proximity of planets can affect stellar winds or small variations in luminosity.
  • Supernova impact on nearby planets
    Research question: What happens to planets when their star explodes as a supernova?
    Method: Place planets at different distances from a star that undergoes a supernova. Observe which planets survive, which orbits change, or which planets are destroyed or ejected.
    Extension: Relate this to real observations of nearby supernovae and investigate the possible effects on habitable zones and the creation of heavy elements.
